Dog communication is the transfer of information between dogs, as well as between dogs and humans.[134] Communication behaviors of dogs include eye gaze, faression,[135][136] vocalization, body posture (including movements of bodies and limbs), and gustatory communication (scents, pheromones, and taste). Dogs mark their territories by urinating on them, which is more likely when entering a new environment.
[137][138] Both sexes of dogs may also urinate to communicate anxiety or frustration, submissiveness, or when in exciting or relaxing situations. [139] Overarousal in dogs can be a result of the dogs' higher cortisol levels.[140] Dogs begin socializing with other dogs by the time they reach the ages of 3 to 8 weeks, and at about 5 to 12 weeks of age, they alter their focus from dogs to humans. [141] Belly exposure in dogs can be a defensive behavior that can lead to a bite or to seek comfort.[142] Humans communicate with dogs by using vocalization, hand signals, and body posture. With their acute sense of hearing, dogs rely on the auditory aspect of communication for understanding and responding to various cues, including the distinctive barking patterns that convey different messages. A study using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has shown that dogs respond to both vocal and nonvocal voices using the brain's region towards the temporal pole, similar to that of humans' brains. Most dogs also looked significantly longer at the face whose expression matched the valence of vocalization.[143][144][145] A study of caudate responses shows that dogs tend to respond more positively to social rewards than to food rewards.[146]

Dogs are domesticated mammals belonging to the family Canidae. They were the very first animals to be domesticated by humans. Evidence shows they evolved from an extinct species of wolf. For thousands of years, they have lived alongside humans. This close relationship earned them the title of man's best friend. They are incredibly diverse in size, shape, and color. A tiny Chihuahua looks completely different from a massive Great Dane. Yet, biologically, they belong to the exact same canine species. Their bodies are built for running, jumping, and exploring. They possess an extraordinary, highly advanced sense of smell. Dogs use their noses to map out the world around them. Their hearing is also far superior to human hearing. They can detect high-frequency sounds that humans cannot notice. Dogs communicate using barks, whines, growls, and body language. A wagging tail usually indicates happiness, excitement, or friendliness. However, it can sometimes signal nervousness or intense tension. They are highly social pack animals by nature. They view their human families as their primary pack. This instinct makes them incredibly loyal and protective companions. Humans have selectively bred dogs for many specific tasks. Working dogs assist people in a massive variety of ways. Herding dogs manage livestock like sheep and cattle efficiently. Guard dogs protect properties from intruders and potential threats. Hunting dogs help track, point, and retrieve wild game. Service dogs offer life-changing independence to disabled individuals. Guide dogs safely navigate the world for the visually impaired. Medical alert dogs can sense oncoming seizures or low blood sugar. Search and rescue dogs track missing people in disasters. Detection dogs sniff out drugs, explosives, and smuggled goods.
